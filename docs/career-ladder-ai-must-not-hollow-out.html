Someone Has to Be New at This

By Josiah Hunter•October 7, 2026•9 min read

As AI changes how we build software, I keep thinking about how we give new engineers the room, support, and responsibility to grow.

It's an awkward thing, being new at something you care about. Picture an engineer making their first small change to an unfamiliar system. They want to be useful. They also have questions they're not quite sure how to ask. A fix looks sensible, the tests pass, and then something happens that they didn't expect.

That moment can feel awful. It can also become the beginning of understanding, especially when someone has time to sit beside them and help work out what happened.

I keep coming back to those moments when I think about AI and junior engineers. A tool can now offer an explanation, suggest a fix, and help someone get past a blank screen. I want people to have that help. Starting out doesn't need to be harder than it already is.

What worries me is how easy it could become to finish the work without taking much of the learning with us. And if fewer people get a chance to start in the first place, there's another problem: the experienced people we hope to rely on later have to come from somewhere.

What the pipeline actually has to preserve

The hiring concern deserves attention, though the evidence calls for care. In an August 2026 update using U.S. payroll data through June, Stanford researchers found that employment among workers ages 22–25 in AI-exposed occupations had fallen behind that of their less-exposed peers. The widening gap appeared mainly in hiring. The researchers describe these findings as early warning signs, rather than proof that AI caused the changes, and they found no evidence of widespread displacement across the economy. (Stanford Digital Economy Lab)

That doesn't tell us what will happen to engineering careers ten years from now. It does make me think about the person sending applications and hoping someone will give them their first real opportunity.

For a company, a junior role can look like work that needs supervision. For the person in that role, it's also a place to become more capable. Those two views belong in the same conversation when we decide what new tools let us change.

Today's senior engineers were beginners once. The route wasn't identical for everyone: people learned through jobs, school, independent projects, and help from others. But experience still means having had opportunities to try things, discover what you misunderstood, and improve. If one route gets narrower, I hope we put some thought into what takes its place.

I'm concerned about a possible future shortage of judgment, not claiming that one has been established. We don't yet have the long-term evidence to say today's hiring changes will leave us without enough senior engineers. Waiting for that outcome to become certain would be a strange time to start caring about how people learn.

Getting help and keeping the understanding

AI can be useful to someone with less experience. Three workplace experiments reported in 2025, involving 4,867 developers at Microsoft, Accenture, and an anonymous Fortune 100 company, found a pooled 26.08% increase in completed tasks with an AI coding assistant. Less-experienced developers saw larger gains and adopted the tool more often. That is encouraging evidence about getting work done; it doesn't by itself tell us what a person can explain or repair afterward. (Management Science)

A smaller study asked a different question. It involved 52 mostly junior engineers learning an unfamiliar Python library, Trio, then taking a quiz without AI. The assisted group averaged 50%, compared with 67% for the group working by hand. Their roughly two-minute speedup wasn't statistically significant. The study measured understanding shortly after the task, so it can't tell us whether that difference lasts over a career. Still, it gives us a reason to pay attention to what someone understands after the work is finished. (Anthropic)

There was something hopeful in that study, too. Among the people using AI, groups who asked conceptual questions, sought explanations, or independently resolved errors averaged at least 65%. Groups who delegated code, became progressively reliant on AI, or relied on it for debugging averaged below 40%. These were small, observational comparisons within the experiment, not proof that any particular habit causes better learning. They suggest that the way we ask for help may matter. (Anthropic)

I can imagine how tempting it is to accept an answer when you're tired, a deadline is close, and you don't want to be the person holding everyone up. It would be unfair to put the whole responsibility for learning on a beginner while measuring them only by how quickly the task disappears from the board.

The team has a part in this. Learning needs some room in the working day. It needs people who can hear “I don't understand this yet” without treating it as a confession of incompetence.

When I talk about going through the difficult parts, I don't mean leaving someone alone with a problem until they feel miserable enough to deserve help. There's nothing admirable about avoidable confusion. The useful part is having a real problem to think about, enough support to make progress, and a chance to understand why the answer works.

Use AI to widen practice, not remove it

I'd start small. Before running a generated change, a junior could say what they expect it to do. After it runs, they could compare that expectation with what happened. If the two don't match, there's something worth talking through.

When a bug appears, perhaps the first step is to reproduce it and write down a guess about its cause. AI can help explore that guess. A mentor can help question it. The important part is that the junior gets to make a guess of their own and learn what the evidence says about it.

Review can make space for that conversation, too. Alongside checking whether a change works, someone can ask how the engineer arrived at it, what they were unsure about, and what they would look at if it failed again. The answer doesn't need to sound polished. A useful review should make it easier to admit where understanding runs out.

I'd also like to see beginners own small pieces of work long enough to live with the result: reading the existing code, making a change, checking the tests against what was actually requested, responding to feedback, and seeing what needs attention later. These are suggestions for how a team might preserve learning, not a tested formula for turning juniors into seniors.

Learning research offers some support for the ingredients behind these suggestions. The National Academies' How People Learn II describes ways technology can support real problems, help along the way, feedback, reflection, and revision. Applying those ideas to an AI-assisted engineering team still means working out what suits the people and the work. (National Academies Press)

For someone starting out, that could mean using AI to ask a question they felt embarrassed to ask aloud, then bringing a better question to a colleague. For a more experienced engineer, it could mean explaining why they distrust the first plausible answer, rather than quietly replacing a junior's work with their own.

One possible review arrangement would have someone check the change's behavior and someone else look at the explanation and risks. They could compare their concerns, work through the changes the evidence supports, and check what actually changed before accepting the result. That would be a team practice to try, rather than a proven teaching method.

Over time, I hope the support changes. The beginner takes on a less familiar problem, makes more of the decisions, and needs less help deciding what to check. They may still use AI constantly. What I care about is whether they understand more than they did before, and whether they can find their footing when the tool gives them an answer that doesn't fit.

The research in a little more detail

The hiring figures describe a particular U.S. payroll panel, not all junior engineers. In the two most AI-exposed groups of occupations, employment among workers ages 22–25 fell about 11% from November 2022 through June 2026; their less-exposed peers saw about 10% growth. The researchers call the difference a 19% “kept-pace” shortfall. The panel isn't nationally representative, some trends predate generative AI, and factors including education mix, interest rates, remote work, and sample composition complicate interpretation. These are descriptive findings, not a settled account of what caused the gap. (Stanford Digital Economy Lab)

There is also reason to resist a story of software work simply disappearing. BLS projects 10% employment growth for software developers, quality assurance analysts, and testers from 2025 to 2035, with about 106,100 openings a year from growth and replacement needs. Its description of the work includes analysis, design, maintenance, testing, security, communication, collaboration, creativity, attention to detail, problem solving, and a strong programming background. It is a broader job than producing code. (BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook)

The speed findings also depend on the setting. In a small experiment involving 16 experienced open-source developers and 246 tasks in familiar, mature projects, early-2025 AI tools made completion 19% slower, despite participants expecting and later perceiving a speedup. That older, specialized result is a useful reminder to examine the work in front of us, rather than assume every team gets the same benefit. (METR)

Another 2026 experiment, involving 69 students, researchers, and professional developers, found more than double the median task completeness with AI assistance, alongside a 12.5% reduction in the ability to answer technical questions about their own implementations. That measure concerns understanding in a bounded task, rather than long-term responsibility for a live system. (IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering)

Developers often say they want to check AI output. In Stack Overflow's 2026 survey, 48.0% of 14,304 respondents trusted it when they could easily verify it, compared with 6.6% trusting it for many tasks including important work decisions. (Stack Overflow Developer Survey)

In a separate question with 13,162 respondents, 76.5% reported running output locally, 63.9% comparing it with the codebase, 53.0% inspecting tests or security implications, and 9.6% using it as-is. These are self-reports from a self-selected survey. They tell us what people say they do, not whether their checks were right or whether they learned from them. (Stack Overflow Developer Survey)

A future with room for beginners

I want these tools to help us go further. I like the thought of people building things that used to feel out of reach, perhaps, one day, even reaching other worlds. I also want the person at the beginning to have a place in that future.

They deserve the chance to become someone who understands what they've built, can help when it breaks, and eventually has the patience to sit beside the next person who's new.
