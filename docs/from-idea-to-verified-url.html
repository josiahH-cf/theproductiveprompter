From Idea to Verified URL: Build a Blog Workflow That Still Sounds Like You

By Josiah Hunter•August 18, 2026•14 min read

Revised October 9, 2026 to reflect the current voice, review and publication workflow.

An AI-assisted article workflow has to keep different kinds of work separate. Someone has to decide what the article is trying to do. The claims need evidence, the writing needs an editor, and publication needs a check of what actually reached the website. Combining all of that in one prompt makes it difficult to tell where a disappointing result came from.

Article Flow leaves an artifact at each boundary: intent, recipe, brief, draft, review, verified claims, package and live result. You can use that approach without copying its whole implementation. Start with one article and make its important decisions inspectable.

The October 9 controller belongs to the 3.2 release, and the authoritative graph declares workflow 3.2.0. This update corrects the August article's statements about model routing and automatic voice learning while retaining its editorial and publication loops.

Start with the finish line

Before choosing a model, prompt library, CMS, or automation service, define the outcome you can inspect.

"Generate an article" is too weak. "Deploy the website" is better, but still incomplete. A useful finish line might say:

  • the approved revision is available at the intended public URL
  • the page renders the correct headline and body in a normal browser
  • the author, dates, summary, image, and canonical URL agree with the article record
  • the blog index and any other internal discovery surfaces link to it
  • the site's chosen sitemap or feed includes the URL when that surface is part of the publication plan
  • the workflow records what revision was deployed, what was checked, and which URL passed

Google's Article guidance calls for preparation, validation, deployment, rendered inspection and sitemap support; it does not guarantee a search feature. Sitemap submission is a hint, not proof that a URL will be crawled. Define the finish line around the page and discovery surfaces you control. Search indexing remains a separate outcome.

Once the finish line is concrete, work backward. What package must exist before publication? What must be true before packaging? What evidence makes a draft ready? What decisions must be owned before drafting? Those answers become the workflow.

Draw two loops, not one giant prompt

Anthropic draws a useful distinction between a workflow, which follows predefined paths, and an agent, which chooses more of its path dynamically. Its recommendation is plain: start with the simplest design that meets the need, then add autonomy only when it creates measurable value.

Article production usually begins as a workflow. The exact research may vary, and a model may help route work, but the major path is predictable enough to draw. The block below is a conceptual overview of the editorial and publication loops, not a complete map of workflow 3.2.0.

A failed check returns the work to the stage that can repair it. Weak evidence goes back to research or claim verification. A reasoning gap goes back to drafting, with development review checking the repair. A generic paragraph goes back to prose work or voice review. A broken link goes back to packaging. A page that deployed but did not render goes back to publication implementation.

The current graph expands this sketch into separately inspectable states. Claims, visuals, public summaries and live delivery have different failure modes; each needs a place to send work that fails.

Keep the editorial and publication loops separate. Their quality is connected, but packaging should begin only after the article's meaning is stable enough that deployment cannot quietly rewrite it.

Make every stage leave something you can inspect

A stage performs work. A gate decides what happens next.

That distinction matters when a workflow fails. "Improve the article until it is good" combines drafting, evaluation, revision, and approval into one foggy instruction. The system can loop forever, declare victory early, or change facts while polishing tone. You cannot tell which part failed because the parts were never separated.

A stage should leave behind an observable artifact. Preserve the seed, turn intent into a brief with supported claims and exclusions, then keep the draft, review and verification records attached to that brief. A failed development review sends a reasoning gap back to drafting before factual locks. Display revision changes the public title and description; packaging propagates them to cards, metadata and the feed. Visual planning owns captions.

In the current path, intent sets the decision, the recipe chooses the article form, the brief locks reader purpose and exclusions, the draft develops the article, development review repairs reasoning, claim, voice and visual controls protect facts, fit and assets, edit changes prose, post-edit verification rechecks changed claims, display revision aligns title and description, editorial QA checks the complete surface, packaging assembles the public candidate, publication executes the scoped approval, and live verification records the exact URL and revision.

Record the workflow and voice-guide versions with each article. When a later revision sounds different or behaves differently, those versions help explain which guidance and controls it actually used.

A useful gate answers seven questions:

  • What artifact is being checked?
  • What conditions count as passing?
  • Who owns any judgment the system cannot settle?
  • Where does the work go when it passes?
  • Which stage can repair each kind of failure?
  • How many attempts are allowed before escalation?
  • What evidence is recorded with the decision?

You do not need dozens of named gates to start. You need enough boundaries that failure has somewhere honest to go. Intent, evidence, development, voice, package, publication, and live result are a practical first set. The names can change. The important part is that each gate looks at evidence and has a consequence. A checklist with no failed path is decoration.

Give development review room to work

A draft can have supported facts and still bury the point, repeat a distinction or skip the mechanism that connects a fact to a decision. Development review comes after the rough draft and before claim verification so those problems can be repaired before the factual surface is locked.

Reverse-outline the draft: write down the job of each section, then check whether it does that job. Two sections that both explain why evidence matters may need to become one. A section that lists controls may need to show what happens when one fails. Keep sources and protected evidence intact while making these changes; then verify the claims in the article the reader will actually receive.

Separate retries from repairs

Some failures deserve another attempt. Others need different work.

A temporary network error may justify a retry. An unsupported claim does not. Repeating the same research step without new direction only spends more time. A voice mismatch needs feedback. An unapproved point of view needs the author. A broken page needs a packaging or rendering fix.

Keep retries bounded, and make publication-side retries especially cautious. If a publish request times out, check whether the first request succeeded before sending it again. Otherwise, a recovery attempt can create duplicate posts, cards, feed entries, or announcements. RFC 9110 makes the underlying distinction for HTTP: automatic repetition is safe only when repeated identical requests have the same intended effect, or when the client knows the original was not applied or the application semantics make the repetition safe.

For subjective repair loops, set an attempt limit. If voice revisions still miss after bounded attempts, more autonomous polishing may compound the problem. Return the disputed passage, alternatives, and passage-specific reasons to the author. Escalation is not workflow failure. It is the workflow recognizing who owns the decision.

Keep voice guidance in the right lane

A voice guide can help from the beginning of a run, but it is not the same thing as human preference evidence.

Article Flow supplies one compact evidence-backed voice guide early. That guide can inform intent, briefing, drafting, and editing. The article-specific contract separately owns the reader, purpose, register, scope, and authorized perspective. This distinction keeps a global guide from swallowing the local job.

Ordinary articles require an actual choice among three local voice options. After that real choice, editing, verification, build, publication, and live verification continue automatically unless a listed stop or explicit hold applies. The choice is local evidence for the current article. It can create a pending scoped proposal, but it does not automatically become a reusable rule.

Reusable guidance needs actual human confirmation with a reason, scope, exceptions, a retained-example check, and a different-form check. The weekly review, when requested, proposes at most one reusable change. It keeps one changed passage, one deliberately retained passage, the author's actual reason, and one development issue, then checks that the proposed rule does not damage retained examples or a different article form.

Delegated unattended revisions are different. When the author has explicitly delegated a bounded revision, the workflow may reuse the pinned guide and bypass only the routine passage choice. That authorization records no new human preference and no likeness rating. The revision still needs evidence, visual, QA, publication, and live checks.

Use examples without pretending they solve identity

Examples can improve visible consistency. They do not prove that a system has captured a person's writing identity.

Anthropic's prompting guidance describes examples as a reliable way to steer output format, tone, and structure, especially when examples are relevant and diverse. That is useful production guidance. It supports giving the model concrete prose to learn from rather than abstract adjectives.

The limit matters too. Wang et al. evaluated personal-style imitation across more than 400 authors and four English-language domains. In that setup, models struggled more with informal blogs and forums, and more demonstrations produced limited gains under the study's metrics. The study did not include large-scale human evaluation, so the narrow lesson is not that models cannot help with voice. The lesson is that examples alone do not establish personal resemblance.

A practical voice gate should report three things separately:

  • voice fit: whether the passage makes choices demonstrated by the guide and the local selection
  • meaning preservation: whether facts, uncertainty, citations, commitments, or the authorized position changed
  • naturalness: whether the passage reads like deliberate prose rather than a rubric converted into sentences

That three-part gate borrows a useful pattern from style-transfer evaluation, where style intensity, content preservation, and naturalness can trade off against each other. It is a practical adaptation, not a universal personal-voice score.

Automated critique can make the loop cheaper, but it should point to passages and explain its judgment. Salemi et al. frame personalized-text evaluation as difficult because the prompt author is the reliable assessor, then propose an explainable reference-based framework rather than eliminating the need for human preference evidence. Research on LLM-as-judge comparisons has also documented position bias. If you use model comparisons, reverse candidate order and require passage-specific reasons. Let the human author settle actual preference.

Successful publication validates the workflow result. It does not prove human resemblance. The author still has to judge whether the voice transfers convincingly to other kinds of writing.

Route work by stage, but keep authority separate

The older version of this article said separate model choice or task switching was still planned. That is now obsolete.

In the October 9 release, the controller implements stage-aware provider routing. It filters configured models by stage and required capabilities, while the run's assigned writing route can remain pinned across writing stages when appropriate. Per-stage routing exists, but that does not mean every stage must use a different model.

Routing is an operational choice, not an authority grant. A stronger model for development review does not get to approve publication. A faster model for formatting does not get to change claims. A private assistant can help with interview, rehearsal, critique, reader simulation, alternatives, and related bounded modes, but assist and evaluate modes cannot advance, publish, or activate a production run.

Source events have the same boundary. A source event can record before-and-after source changes and identify public surfaces that may be affected. It is a semantic-review hypothesis. It does not authorize publication, scheduling, or reusable guidance. It tells the workflow where attention may be needed; it does not decide that the affected article should go live.

These boundaries keep automation useful. The model can do work. The controller owns state transitions. The article contract owns local reader purpose and authorized perspective. The human author owns unresolved preference, lived-experience claims, and reusable voice confirmation. The publication policy owns where a scoped approval may publish.

Treat the project history as dated evidence

The Productive Prompter's 31 Days experiment already solved meaningful pieces of this system. The public Day 31 retrospective reports bulk article generation and scheduling, daily and then weekly specification changes, and the cost of reading, fact-checking, tone, and human review. Those are dated observations from the December 2025 project, not general measured results about every workflow.

The site history also matters. The legacy shared article viewer had an initial HTML shell with a generic title, then used JavaScript to fetch Markdown, inject the rendered body, and update the document title. Google documents crawling, rendering, and indexing as distinct phases for JavaScript pages, so a JavaScript shell is not proof that a page is absent from Google. It does explain why rendered inspection matters.

The dedicated article page is different. Its source contains the article identity, canonical URL, first-publication timestamp, Article metadata, and body in the initial HTML response. That makes the page identity and content available for direct source inspection, with browser rendering still to be checked.

The completed 31 Days of AI series remains visible at its existing archive and article links. This correction does not redesign that archive or convert its dated observations into new experiment results. It uses the project as evidence for why inspectable stages, gates, and publication checks matter.

Publish only what was approved, then verify what is live

Packaging, approval, publication, and live verification are separate states.

Packaging gathers the article body, metadata, links, discovery surfaces, structured data, visual assets, and any platform-specific files into a public candidate. Approval is scoped. In the current workflow, ordinary policy approval is limited to a fixed allowlisted target unless a hold is active. Publication executes that approved package. Live verification checks the exact rendered revision and public surfaces after execution.

That separation prevents a common mistake: treating access as approval. WordPress, for example, exposes post statuses such as publish, future, draft, pending, and private. Those statuses are platform outputs. They are not the workflow's editorial source of truth. The workflow should know which revision was approved before any platform status changes.

Live verification should check the exact page, not merely the existence of a deployment. Inspect the rendered headline and body. Check the canonical URL, dates, description, structured data, links, and discovery surfaces that the publication plan controls. Confirm that the live page corresponds to the deployed revision rather than an older or concurrent run. Record the URL that passed.

Record the verified URL and revision. Keep search indexing outside that delivery claim.

Try it with one article

You can design the first version on one page. Write the public finish line, the artifacts needed to reach it, and the owner of each consequential decision. For each gate, give the work somewhere to go when it fails and set an attempt limit.

Then run one article through it. Keep the original seed, reader purpose and authorized perspective visible through drafting. Check the development before locking claims. Review the body and its public summary together. Publish the scoped package, inspect the actual URL, and record which revision passed. If a step fails, follow its repair path rather than restarting the whole article or repeating an uncertain publication action.

Before you call the run done, add two closing checks: confirm whether any voice evidence is local to the article, pending as a proposal, or reusable only after human confirmation; and record that live verification is delivery evidence while search indexing remains outside the pass claim.

Use the first run to locate one weak boundary. If the draft repeats itself, improve development review. If a diagram contradicts the explanation, return to visual planning. If the wrong revision reaches the website, strengthen the publication evidence. Make that correction, then carry its record into the next article.
