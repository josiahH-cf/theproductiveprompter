What Prompt Precision Bought in 24 Agent Trials

By Josiah Hunter•October 2, 2026•28 min read

TLDR

  • Observed: All 24 trials met the intended specification on the first pass. Spelling errors, reduced grammar and ambiguous rules produced no observed outcome difference.
  • Observed: Ambiguous prompts used 9% more mean output for Task A and 27% more for Task B.
  • Interpretation: On these tasks, stating the governing rule mattered more than polishing the English. The results do not show that ambiguity is harmless.

Observed: This was a small exploratory experiment: two synthetic tasks, four prompt conditions, and three repetitions for each task-condition pairing. That adds up to 24 trials of claude-sonnet-5-5 through Claude Code 2.1.287 at high effort. The models used later to write and review the article were separate from the models being tested. Task A summarized orders by region; Task B repaired a billing-proration function. Independent validators checked the intended answers, and every run executed its provided check after its final file change.

Observed: All six spelling-error runs succeeded. About one in three ordinary words was misspelled, while identifiers stayed intact. Mean output was within 2% of clear in both tasks. Tool and turn counts were broadly similar, with one extra tool call in a clear Task A run. Interpretation: This model recovered the tested words from context; the experiment did not test misspellings that change an identifier, number or negation.

Observed: All six reduced-grammar runs also succeeded. Removing punctuation, capitalization and function words coincided with 5% more mean output for Task A and 18% more for Task B. One early Task B run made an extra tool call and accounted for most of that task's gap. Interpretation: These prompts remained understandable, but the design did not isolate a causal grammar effect.

Observed: All six ambiguous runs selected the intended rules: completed orders, normalized statuses and deduplicated records for Task A; inclusive days and half-up rounding for Task B. No ambiguous final message asked a question. Four broadly coded messages flagged a judgment or explained a choice beyond the tests. Interpretation: The selected conventional defaults matched the intended answers. Unconventional intent was not tested, so a correct result here does not establish that a vague rule is safe.

Calculated estimate: The total API-equivalent estimate was $0.589, and the clear-condition mean per trial was $0.0241, using official Claude pricing retrieved on 2026-10-02. These are estimates, never charges; the runs used a subscription. Interpretation: Cache-write timing appears to drive most of the cost spread. The earliest Task A run in each condition had the most writes, but the experiment did not isolate timing and wording as causes.

Interpretation: Spend precision first on the rule, exceptions and success criteria. Keep spelling and grammar clear enough to communicate them. Observed: The measurement gaps remain: no billed cost, subscription usage not separable per trial, sampling settings not exposed by the CLI, three repetitions per cell, and an all-success ceiling that prevented detection of outcome differences. Conclusions apply only to this model, Claude Code version, these two tasks and these variants. Exact prompts, every repetition, usage, estimates, validations and labeled excerpts appear in the technical evidence.

Additional condition comparisons and calculation assumptions

The experiment

Observed: The experiment was deliberately small: two synthetic tasks, four prompt conditions, and three repetitions for each task-condition pairing. That adds up to 24 trials of claude-sonnet-5-5 through Claude Code 2.1.287 at high effort. The models used later to write and review the article were separate from the models being tested.

Observed: Task A created a regional summary from order and exchange-rate files. Task B repaired a proration function. Independent validators checked the intended answers, including requirements that the visible checks did not cover. Every Task A run executed check_format.py, and every Task B run executed pytest. The complete prompts, results, usage records, commands, diffs, and excerpts appear in the technical evidence.

Spelling

Observed: All six spelling-error prompts succeeded. Mean output was 1,277 tokens for Task A, compared with 1,254 for the clear prompts, and 693 for Task B. Both spelling means were within 2% of clear. Tool and turn counts were also comparable, and no run mentioned the misspellings.

Interpretation: This model read through the tested misspellings. The result does not cover errors in identifiers, paths, numbers, negations, or domain terms, where a typo could change the instruction itself.

Grammar

Observed: All six reduced-grammar prompts succeeded. Mean output for Task A was 1,317 tokens, 5% higher than clear. Mean output for Task B was 18% higher than clear.

Observed: One early Task B trial produced 996 output tokens after making an extra Bash call. The other two produced 732 and 740, so that run accounts for most of the Task B gap.

Interpretation: Polished grammar was not necessary for these prompts, but the experiment did not establish that reduced grammar caused more output.

Goal clarity

Observed: The ambiguous prompts replaced explicit governing rules with vague instructions. Task A used:

Leave out any orders that shouldn't count.

Task B used:

Make sure the amounts come out right.

All six still reached the intended answer by choosing completed orders, normalized statuses, deduplicated records, inclusive billing days, and half-up rounding.

Observed: Four of the six final messages flagged a judgment or explained a choice beyond the tests. None asked for clarification.

Interpretation: The agents succeeded because their conventional defaults matched the intended rules. Unconventional intent was not tested. A vague prompt can therefore produce a correct result while still leaving an important decision to the agent.

Tokens, estimates, and limits

Observed: Ambiguous prompts produced 1,370 mean output tokens for Task A, 9% more than clear, and 27% more for Task B. Their final messages were also longer, although the experiment did not identify which tokens were explanatory or prove that ambiguity caused the difference.

Calculated estimate: The 24 trials totaled $0.589 in API-equivalent estimates, not billed charges. The clear-condition mean was $0.0241, and the ambiguous-condition mean was $0.0242. Condition means ranged from $0.0241 to $0.0257, while individual trials ranged from $0.0131 to $0.0369. Mean cost per successful Task A completion reached $0.0334, and the Task B range was $0.0155 to $0.0202, depending on condition. A projection for 1,000 comparable tasks was about $0.024–$0.026 per successful task. The additional ambiguous-condition output was estimated at about $0.001–$0.002 per task.

Calculated estimate: The calculation used the official Claude pricing retrieved on 2026-10-02. These were API-equivalent estimates rather than charges because the trials ran through a subscription session.

Interpretation: Cache-write timing appears important because cache writes varied from 492 to 4,376 tokens and the earliest Task A run in each condition had the most. The design did not isolate cache timing, turn count, or wording as causes.

Calculated estimate: The 24-of-24 result is still uncertain. With six successes in six trials per condition, the one-sided 95% exact binomial lower confidence bound is 0.05^(1/6) = 0.606962 under independent trials with a common success probability. It is not a guaranteed real-world bound.

Interpretation: Apply the result narrowly. State governing rules, exceptions, invariants, and validation criteria before polishing every sentence. Improve spelling and grammar when they obscure meaning. Do not rely on a conventional default when a wrong assumption would be expensive or difficult to detect.

Interpretation: The evidence covers one model, one harness, two synthetic tasks, four variants, and three repetitions per cell. Sampling settings were not exposed, subscription use could not be allocated per trial, and the 24-of-24 result created a ceiling effect. No general robustness or causal cost claim follows from it.
