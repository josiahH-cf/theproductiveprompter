What I Mean by AI Alignment and Why I Expect It to Improve

By Josiah Hunter•September 22, 2026•3 min read

When an AI fixes a repository issue and the tests pass, it is easy to feel that the job is done. The tests only tell us what they tested, so I keep coming back to a simpler question: did the AI act as intended?

SWE-bench makes part of that question concrete. It gives a system a software issue and repository context, applies the proposed patch, and runs tests. A passing result is evidence under those checks. It does not establish that the checks captured everything the person wanted.

My working definition of alignment starts with that question. I would judge whether the AI acted as intended by measured results relative to the goal it was meant to pursue. For a particular goal, alignment is a statistic representing the percentage of occasions on which an AI acts in accordance with it. It does so only some of the time, rather than on every occasion.

I do not have a general percentage to report. I have not specified a general method for measuring it, named a dataset that establishes a broad trend, or established the cause of that trend. Still, I believe the share of cases in which AI acts in accordance with our goals is gradually increasing. That belief is why I expect alignment to improve in the future, though it is not a guarantee.

Bounded benchmarks offer more specific evidence. In an April 14, 2025 report, OpenAI reported that GPT-4.1 resolved 54.6% of its evaluated SWE-bench Verified tasks, compared with 33.2% for GPT-4o. OpenAI omitted 23 of the benchmark's 500 tasks because their solutions could not run on its infrastructure, and said performance depended strongly on prompts and tools.

Those results describe a particular evaluation setup. They are useful task evidence, not a universal alignment percentage.

The same report gave GPT-4.1 an IFEval score of 87.4%, compared with 81.0% for GPT-4o. IFEval checks verifiable natural-language requirements, including word-count and keyword constraints. It can establish compliance with included rules while leaving open whether the response fully satisfied the person's intent.

I find it useful to separate four things: the human goal, the AI's interpretation of that goal, the action it takes, and the result an evaluator can check. They are related, but a test supplies evidence only about the conditions it includes. Requirements outside those conditions remain untested.

Tests also deserve scrutiny. In a February 2026 audit, OpenAI found some tests rejecting valid solutions in a selected difficult subset. It also reported evidence of exposure to benchmark material in the frontier models it tested. The audit does not establish the error rate for the whole benchmark or determine whether GPT-4.1's earlier result was contaminated. A score should be read alongside test quality, task selection, and possible prior exposure.

Generated explanations have a similar limit. A model may offer a useful account of its answer, but that account is not guaranteed to faithfully identify what influenced it. In 2023 experiments, researchers found cases where biasing features affected answers without appearing in generated explanations. Behavior and explanation should both be checked.

There is a narrower reason for optimism. The InstructGPT study found that fine-tuning models with human demonstrations and preference feedback improved human-rated outputs on the study's prompt distribution. That does not establish the cause of later benchmark scores or a general trend. It does show that targeted training and feedback can improve measured behavior.

For everyday work, I would call repeated success on relevant tasks practical reliability. Better feedback, clearer goals, and stronger checks can plausibly improve it, provided the checks reflect what people need and the gains carry over to new situations.

My impression is that people often use alignment more broadly than practical reliability. They may mean an interpretable mission that includes the human goal, the AI's interpretation of it, and how it acts. They may also expect an AI to anticipate when following an instruction would be unsafe and explicitly refuse it.

That differs from merely failing to finish a task. An inability to complete a request may be a capability failure. An explicit refusal is a deliberate response to a relevant boundary.

I have not settled whether acting as intended is enough to define alignment, or whether alignment should also include the capacity to explicitly refuse unsafe instructions.
