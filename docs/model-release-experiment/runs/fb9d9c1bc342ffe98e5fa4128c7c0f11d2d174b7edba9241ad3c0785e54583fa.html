1. THE STORY

Short Run

The Risograph had been failing politely for three weeks — a hitch in the drum, a smell like hot pennies when it ran long — and the quote taped to the corkboard said $220, Friday, no extensions. It was Thursday. The shop smelled like ink and the inside of a radiator.

At nine that morning Mara had counted the tin: eighty dollars, soft bills, one of them taped. Eighty dollars of paper and masters, a hundred and twenty minutes on the drum before she'd promised the repair tech she'd stop, and eight jobs on the clipboard, each one yes-or-no, no halves. Customers paid on pickup, which meant tonight, which meant this afternoon's paper had to come out of the tin or not at all.

She'd worked it out twice with a pencil on a scrap of newsprint and then written it on the whiteboard in her squarest capitals: POSTERS. TICKETS. MENUS. BANNERS. Eighty dollars of materials exactly. A hundred and twenty minutes exactly. Two hundred ninety-four dollars coming back, two hundred fourteen over the paper cost, and then the thirty-five for the month's electric and the dumpster, which left one seventy-nine toward the drum.

Ivo had come in at ten and seen BANNERS on the board and actually put his hand flat on it, the way you'd touch a dog.

"You put the theater first."

"The theater priced out first," Mara said. "Don't get sentimental. It's the biggest single job on the list."

He made coffee that tasted like a penny too, and squared the banner stock on the long table, and hummed something from the show, and at eleven-forty Ruth from the Tuesday collective came in holding a receipt and looking apologetic in the way that meant money.

Fourteen dollars. The replacement blade for the guillotine cutter, which had been sharpened two weeks ago, which Mara had told her to go ahead and handle, which Ruth had handled out of the tin. Before any of it. Before a single sheet was bought.

So: sixty-six dollars.

Mara sat down on the stool with the newsprint scrap and did it again. She was aware of Ivo behind her, not breathing much.

Banners took thirty dollars of paper. Thirty out of sixty-six was almost half the shop's whole afternoon spent on one job, and the money came back fine, it always came back fine, but fine wasn't the question anymore. The question was what sixty-six dollars could carry.

She wrote the new board: POSTERS. PROGRAMS. TICKETS. BOOKMARKS. Sixty-six dollars of materials. A hundred minutes.

"A hundred," Ivo said. "You've got twenty minutes sitting there doing nothing."

"I know."

"The drum's dying tomorrow and you're going to let it idle for twenty minutes."

"Minutes aren't the wall," she said. "Paper's the wall. I could stand here with four hours and it wouldn't buy me a sheet."

"Then run the banners instead of the bookmarks and the posters—"

"I did that. On paper. Banners, programs, tickets — that's sixty-four dollars, a hundred five minutes, and it comes in at a hundred forty-one after the thirty-five." She held the scrap up like a receipt. "This board comes in at a hundred forty-four."

Ivo looked at it a long moment. "Three dollars."

"Three dollars."

"Mara. Three dollars is not a reason. That theater let us use their lobby when the roof went. Dana's been handing out our flyers at intermission for a year."

"I know what Dana's done."

"Then act like it."

"I am," she said. "The gap's seventy-six dollars either way we're short, and I'm not going to be short and have picked the worse number on purpose. If it survives, we print their banners Monday and I'll eat the master."

"If."

"If."

He went and unsquared the banner stock, slid it back into the flat file, and the drawer shut with a sound like the cutter. Then he inked the drum for the posters anyway, because he was that kind of angry.

They ran until four-ten. Posters, programs, tickets, bookmarks. Her hands were cyan to the second knuckle. Payments came in as people came in — two hundred forty-five dollars, and sixty-six of it was already paper, and thirty-five of it was already the dumpster and the lights, and what sat in the tin at the end, clean, countable, new, was a hundred forty-four dollars.

Seventy-six short.

At five, Theo from the co-op stuck his head in and said he'd talked to someone who might put in a hundred tonight, might, he'd know by nine.

"Might," Mara said, and wrote $144 on the board, and under it, $76 TO GO, and nothing about Theo at all.

They cleaned the rollers. The phone didn't ring. Ivo stayed to sweep, which was how she knew it would be all right between them eventually, and at six they locked up with a hundred forty-four dollars in the tin and no gift in it.
