## 1. THE STORY

### Twenty Minutes of Quiet

At three o’clock, the cutter made a noise like a spoon caught in a drain.

“Don’t,” Mara told it.

The cutter stopped. Beyond the front window, afternoon sunlight illuminated the paper dust over the community print shop’s counter. Someone had taped a child’s drawing of the building to the glass. In the drawing, the shop had a chimney and smoke shaped like hearts.

Ivo stood beneath it holding the theater’s banner proof.

“They’ve already hung the ropes,” he said.

Mara had eighty dollars for materials, two hours of machine time, and a repair deadline that evening. The repair company needed another $220. After today, without the repair, the shop would close.

“The ropes don’t make it cheaper.”

“It’s their first show since the flood.”

She knew. Ivo had spent six Saturdays digging mud from the theater basement. He still wore the ruined shoes.

Eight order slips lay on the counter. Mara moved them with a blunt pencil, adding figures on the backs of spoiled wedding invitations. Ivo kept the banner proof upright, as though it were a person waiting to be introduced.

Finally she tapped four slips.

“Posters. Tickets. Menus. Your banners.”

His shoulders dropped.

“Eighty in materials,” she said. “Every minute we’ve got. Two hundred ninety-four coming back. Less eighty, that’s two hundred fourteen. Then thirty-five to operate today. One hundred seventy-nine for the repair.”

“Forty-one short.”

“Yes.”

“But the banners work.”

“They work,” she agreed.

For a moment, they were on the same side of the counter.

Then Mara opened the cash tin.

She counted twice, smoothing each bill against her apron. Under the tray lay a washer, three foreign coins, and the tiny brass key nobody could identify.

“Sixty-six.”

“You said eighty.”

“I know what I said.”

She checked the envelope where she had written the available float. Eighty, in her own handwriting. Inside was sixty-six. Whatever mistake she had made, the paper supplier would not accept her handwriting for the difference.

Nothing had been purchased. Nothing had run.

Ivo lowered the proof.

“Keep the banners. Change the others.”

Mara began again.

The shop smelled of warm rubber and the orange Ivo had peeled over the wastebasket. Through the wall came the muffled thumping of someone upstairs practicing a dance.

“With banners,” she said eventually, “the best we can do is banners, programs, tickets. Sixty-four in materials. One hundred seventy-six before operating.”

“That sounds all right.”

“Posters, programs, tickets, bookmarks gives us one hundred seventy-nine.”

He stared at her.

“Three dollars?”

“Yes.”

“You’re dropping the theater for three dollars?”

She pressed the pencil so hard its point snapped.

“I’m dropping the banners because we need seventy-six dollars even after taking the best work available. I can’t make the gap bigger because I like your people.”

“They’re your people too.”

That was the thing that hurt.

Mara turned the banner proof toward herself. Ivo had drawn the lettering by hand before scanning it. The long tail of the final letter became a river.

“They are,” she said.

Neither spoke until the upstairs footsteps stopped.

“Someone said a donor might give a hundred tonight,” Ivo offered.

“Has anyone received it?”

“No.”

“Has anyone promised?”

“No. Someone’s asking.”

Mara set the proof on the shelf above the telephone, where it would stay clean.

Ivo called the theater himself.

Then he carried the accepted slips to the worktable.

They bought sixty-six dollars of stock. The posters emerged first, damp-looking black against yellow. Programs followed, then tickets, then bookmarks with a crooked little owl that made Ivo smile despite himself.

At a hundred machine minutes, Mara switched off the press.

“We’ve got twenty left,” Ivo said.

She nodded. Their materials money was spent. Payments would come after production ended; they could not turn those payments into another run.

He swept while she collected the $245, restored the $66 float, and set aside the fixed $35 expense. She put the remaining $144 in the repair envelope and wrote **76 STILL NEEDED** beneath it.

No donation had arrived. No commitment had come.

At closing, Mara turned the sign around. Ivo reached past her to straighten the child’s drawing.

“Leave that up,” he said.

She did.
