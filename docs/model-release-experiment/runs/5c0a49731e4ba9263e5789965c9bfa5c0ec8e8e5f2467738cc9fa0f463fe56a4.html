1. THE STORY

The Color of What We Can Prove

By three o’clock, the print shop smelled like warm toner, damp wool, and the lemon oil Mara used on the front counter when she was nervous. The storm had followed customers in all day, leaving gray half-moons on the linoleum and little beads of water along the edges of the sample books. Outside, October leaned on the windows. Inside, the cutter flashed under the fluorescent lights like a small guillotine.

Mara had written the repair deadline on the brown paper taped above the press:

FRIDAY, 5 PM. NEW MONEY NEEDED: $220.

Under it, in smaller letters:

No new rollers, no shop.

Ivo stood beneath the sign with a theater banner proof in both hands. It was for the community players’ revival of Our Town, though the proof made Grover’s Corners look less like a town and more like a warning.

“They open tomorrow,” he said. “If we don’t run the banners, they hang photocopies in the lobby. Do you want the last thing we do here to be stiffing the theater?”

Mara was sorting job envelopes into two piles. Her pencil was behind one ear. Her hair had come loose from its clip and was stuck, strand by strand, to her cheek. “I want the last thing we do here to be paying the repair bill.”

“That’s a cold sentence.”

“It’s a heated sentence. It came directly from the fuser.”

He did not laugh. Ivo had been volunteering at the shop since the spring, mostly because the theater rehearsed across the alley and he believed in community in the dangerous way people believed in weather: sincerely, but without calculating the cost of umbrellas.

Mara did calculate. She had been doing it all afternoon on the back of a misprinted zoning flyer. Eight orders waited in the rack: posters, programs, tickets, postcards, bookmarks, menus, theater banners, and maps. Each customer would pay after pickup, and not a minute before. The shop had eighty dollars left for paper, ink, and stock, and two hours of machine time before the technician’s cutoff. The repair fund needed two hundred twenty dollars in new money. The old materials float did not count. The press did not care about hope.

“We can do four jobs,” Mara said. “Posters, programs, tickets, postcards. A, B, C, D.”

Ivo looked past her at the banner envelope marked G. “Not the theater.”

“Not under the best plan.”

“Best for who?”

“For the bill.” She tapped the figures. “Materials: eighteen, twenty-four, ten, twenty-eight. Exactly eighty. Time: twenty-five, forty, fifteen, thirty-five. One hundred fifteen minutes. They pay sixty-eight, ninety, thirty-eight, ninety-six. Two hundred ninety-two total. Subtract the eighty in materials and we raise two hundred twelve. Then the thirty-five operating expense comes out. One hundred seventy-seven toward repairs.”

“One hundred seventy-seven isn’t two-twenty.”

“No. It leaves forty-three.”

“So include the banners. They pay one-twelve.”

“They also take thirty dollars and fifty minutes. If we put them in, something better has to come out.”

“Better than saving face?”

Mara set down the pencil. The press behind them clicked as it cooled, patient as a locked door. She could see Ivo’s hurt, and because she liked him, it irritated her. The theater had given the shop posters to print when Mara’s mother still owned the place. Actors had stood in this room wearing winter coats over costumes, begging for last-minute changes. She knew the shape of loyalty. She also knew the shape of an unpaid invoice.

“Ivo,” she said, softer. “If feelings were receivable, I’d staple them to the bill.”

The bell over the door gave a weak jangle. Nessa from the bakery came in, rain glittering on her glasses. Mara expected another envelope, another plea, but Nessa held out a bank pouch.

“I’m sorry,” she said. “I got the deposit wrong. The cash I promised you for stock? It’s sixty-six, not eighty. I counted the farmers’ market drawer twice.”

For a second no one spoke. Then the shop seemed to shrink. The paper stacks looked taller. The big Heidelberg in the back looked older. Even the lemon smell turned sharp.

Ivo said, almost eagerly, “Then the old plan’s gone anyway.”

Mara took the pouch. Her fingers felt stupid and cold. “Yes.”

“So maybe now the banners fit.”

“Maybe.” She pulled the paper close again and crossed out the eighty. “Sixty-six for materials. Same two hours. Same rule: payments come after production.”

Ivo leaned over the counter. Rain ticked at the window. Somewhere in the back, the wall clock made every second sound like a coin dropping into someone else’s jar.

Mara worked the numbers twice, then a third time because dread made liars of arithmetic. With sixty-six dollars, the old group was impossible. Posters, programs, tickets, postcards had needed the full eighty. The theater banners could combine with programs and tickets: thirty plus twenty-four plus ten in materials, one hundred five minutes, one hundred seventy-six in surplus before operating costs. Good. Painfully good.

But posters, programs, tickets, and bookmarks used exactly sixty-six dollars too: eighteen, twenty-four, ten, fourteen. One hundred minutes. Their payments totaled two hundred forty-five. Materials came to sixty-six. Surplus: one hundred seventy-nine. After the operating expense, one hundred forty-four for the repair.

Mara circled A, B, C, E.

Ivo saw it before she said it. His mouth closed around whatever argument had been waiting.

“It beats the banner plan by three dollars,” she said.

“Three dollars.”

“Yes.”

“You’re going to tell them no over three dollars?”

“I’m going to tell them no because three dollars is confirmed and the banner order is not the maximizing plan.”

He laughed once, flatly. “That sounds like something a machine would say.”

Mara’s eyes stung, which annoyed her more than his accusation. She turned the job envelopes around so the labels faced him. “This machine is running out of afternoon. The repair guy won’t take sincerity. He won’t take a possible donor. He won’t take the theater being important, even though it is.”

At that, Ivo looked away.

Behind him, the banner proof bent slightly under his thumb. Its ink was only a simulation, not yet real. A purple sky, a white church, a line of dates. It would have looked good in the lobby. Not necessary, maybe, but good in the way things were good when a town wanted to recognize itself.

“Marcy said someone might give a hundred tonight,” he said.

“Might?”

“She sounded hopeful.”

“Did she have the money?”

“No.”

“Did anyone commit it?”

“No.”

Mara folded the revised list and taped it beside the repair note. Her handwriting looked harsher than she felt.

Ivo was quiet long enough that Mara began feeding the poster stock herself. Then he came around the counter and lifted the box of program paper.

“Bookmarks next?” he asked.

“After tickets.”

“The theater’s going to be angry.”

“Yes.”

“With both of us?”

Mara looked at him. The shop lights hummed. His shoulders had dropped, but he was still there, holding the paper.

“If you stay,” she said, “probably.”

He nodded and loaded the tray. They worked without much talk. Posters came out smelling of ink and heat. Tickets fell into neat white stacks, perforated and bright. Programs clicked through the folder with a sound like cards being shuffled by someone careful. The bookmarks, small and glossy, flashed blue and green under the drying rack.

At five minutes to five, Mara shut the press down. The theater envelope still sat by the register, untouched. Ivo picked it up, then laid it flat and wrote across the front: DECLINED TODAY. NO MATERIALS PURCHASED.

It was kinder than Mara would have managed.

By closing, the customers had come and paid. Mara counted the new money twice: one hundred forty-four after the operating expense, exactly as the pencil had promised and exactly not enough. The gap was seventy-six. No donor came. No message arrived. The storm cleared without ceremony, leaving the alley washed and shining.

Mara locked the door while Ivo taped a note to the glass. He had written it in block letters, steady and legible.

“Will it help?” he asked.

“It’s the truth,” Mara said.

They stood together on the wet sidewalk, looking at the notice from outside. Inside, the shop was dim except for the repair sign and the unfinished banner proof, purple as evening, waiting for a tomorrow that had not been paid for.
