Ads Are the Goal, Not the Goal?

By Josiah Hunter•October 28, 2026•11 min read

When I search for something on Google, I usually know where I'm trying to go. Dotloop is my example. I search for it, the paid ads come first, and the thing I actually wanted is usually the third or fourth thing down. It's a small delay, and it still grates, because I came for one thing and had to get past several others to reach it.

Those searches show what it was like to look for a particular site and have to get past the ads first. The exact queries, dates, devices, markets, and ad counts aren't available here, so this isn't a test of Search as a whole or the quality of its results.

The friction still points at a real tension. In a search like this, the person is trying to reach a particular destination. An ad is there to win that person's attention for something an advertiser paid to show. Sometimes those line up, and the ad is the destination or a reasonable substitute for it. When they don't, the most prominent part of the page is serving a different goal than the one the person brought to it. That's the question behind the title: when ads become the most prominent thing on the page, or interrupt the thing someone came to watch, are they supporting the product's purpose or becoming the purpose?

What Google says the top of the page is doing

Google's own documentation describes something more variable than "ads always go first." Its Search Network help page says Search ads can appear above or below the unpaid results and are labeled with terms such as "Sponsored," "Ad," or "Ads." Its Search consumer information page says Google shows ads only when it believes they're relevant, and adds: "If we have no relevant ads, we show no ads at all." By Google's account, then, not every query carries ads, and an ad's spot on the page isn't fixed.

Where an ad lands comes out of an auction, and the auction isn't a straight sale of the top spot. Google's Ad Rank documentation lists the factors that decide whether an ad is eligible and where it shows: the advertiser's bid, the quality of the ad and the page it links to, minimum thresholds, how competitive the auction is, the context of the search, the person's location and device, the time, and the other ads and results on the page. In working terms, paying is one input. The rest of that list helps decide how far up the page an ad lands, or whether it shows at all.

Google also draws a line between that auction and the unpaid results. The same consumer information page says Google never accepts payment for inclusion in search results or for a higher ranking in them. That's Google's statement about its own system, not an independently audited finding. If it holds, the ads above my Dotloop searches weren't changing how the unpaid results were ordered. They were occupying the space above them.

Google has also changed how that space is presented. In October 2025 it announced a Search design that groups text ads under one persistent "Sponsored results" label, adds a control to hide the group, and caps a grouping at four text ads. Google described a global rollout on desktop and mobile, but whether a given person sees that design on a given search can vary. These are Google's documented rules; they can't establish what was on my screen during those searches.

The ad halfway through the video

YouTube gave me a sharper version of the same feeling. About halfway through watching a video, an ad showed up that seemed to take up half the screen, and it literally overrode my attention on the video. I was there to watch one thing, and for that stretch the ad was what I was watching.

"Half the screen" is an impression, not a measurement, and the episode comes without a date, a device, a surface, a video length, or a named format. That missing detail matters, because YouTube documents several different ways an ad can show up during viewing. Its mid-roll help page describes ads that run during monetized videos at least eight minutes long, in slots a creator can place manually or let YouTube place automatically, and it notes that a slot doesn't guarantee an ad will run. Its advertising formats page describes "Watch While" ads that appear alongside a video on mobile without pausing it and can be closed or left to dismiss after 30 seconds. The same page says YouTube stopped running its legacy desktop overlay ads on April 6, 2023. The two details from that episode, its timing and its apparent size, don't settle which of these it was, so the format stays unnamed here.

Research can't tell me which ad I saw, but it does say something about timing. A mid-roll ad breaks into a video that's already playing, and a pre-roll ad plays before it starts. In a peer-reviewed online experiment with 327 participants, mid-roll ads produced more perceived intrusiveness and more anger than pre-roll ads. In a 2024 CHI study of a YouTube viewing experiment with 45 participants, experiences were more negative when the ad came mid-roll, and 13 of the 15 participants offered a choice of placement picked pre-roll. Both studies are bounded: one is a single online experiment, the other is a small study with a narrow test setup, and neither speaks for viewers in general. Within those limits, they point to one useful thing: an ad can feel more intrusive depending on where it lands inside the thing someone came to watch.

The CHI interviews supported that pattern, but the study's corrected quantitative tests weren't statistically significant. Its participants were a local convenience sample, and the ad was inserted into one short video under test conditions.

Why the incentive is real, and where it stops

Alphabet's 2025 Form 10-K shows why advertising sits so close to the center of these products. For the year ended December 31, 2025, Alphabet reported $224.532 billion in revenue from Google Search & other, $40.367 billion from YouTube ads, $294.691 billion from Google advertising overall, and $402.836 billion in total revenue. "Google Search & other" is Alphabet's category name; it covers Google's search properties plus other Google-owned properties such as Gmail, Google Maps and Google Play, so it's more than the search box. Dividing Google advertising revenue by total revenue puts advertising at about 73% of everything Alphabet brought in that year.

That figure speaks to one side of the tension, the financial incentive to monetize attention, and not to the experiences themselves. When roughly three of every four revenue dollars come from advertising, attention on a results page or partway through a video is worth real money, and the incentive to monetize it runs across the products. Alphabet's filing credits part of the growth in Search & other to advertiser spending and to improvements in ad formats and delivery.

The revenue mix shows the financial pressure on these products. It doesn't explain the decisions behind a particular Search layout or YouTube ad, or establish that Google's goal is advertising rather than helping people search or watch videos.

The evidence for the ads

There is also evidence that search ads can help users. A randomized field experiment with 3.3 million U.S. users of an anonymous search engine made ads less prominent for some users. Both revenue and overall use of the search engine fell. The authors concluded that, at the margin they tested, the ads were making consumers better off. In plain terms, at that level of prominence, the ads appear to have been doing useful work for the people searching.

The researchers tested one change in prominence on an unnamed search engine, and frequent users were excluded after an error in how users were randomized. The finding applies to that specific change; it doesn't say every ad or placement helps. It does make a purely anti-ad conclusion hard to defend, and it fits something ordinary about searching: sometimes the ad really is the place a person was trying to go.

The same question, asked of an AI assistant

I have the same concern about AI assistants: advertising may become part of the interaction, and the pattern could intensify. ChatGPT gives us one dated case to examine, with reasons to take that concern seriously and reasons to doubt it's inevitable. It doesn't stand in for every assistant, and the concern remains a hypothesis about what could happen.

OpenAI began testing ads in ChatGPT on February 9, 2026, for logged-in adults in the U.S. on its Free and Go plans, and an August 11 update to that page reported launches in the UK, Mexico, Brazil, Japan and South Korea. OpenAI's Ads in ChatGPT help page, as checked on October 28, 2026, says Free and Go may show ads while Plus, Pro, Business, Enterprise and Edu don't, and that availability varies by region and over time. Go is a paid plan, so paying for ChatGPT doesn't by itself mean seeing no ads.

On the question that matters most, OpenAI's testing page is direct: "Ads do not influence the answers ChatGPT gives you." The help page adds that advertisers can't alter responses, that ads are labeled, and that "Ads run on separate systems from our chat model." Those are OpenAI's statements about its own system. An independent 2026 preprint used 91 simulated user accounts to collect more than 3,000 ads and found them clearly separated from the response text in its sample, but it didn't test whether advertising affected how answers were generated. OpenAI's January 2026 announcement, written before testing began, described planned sponsored placements at the bottom of answers. That was a plan at the time, not a description of every placement now.

Placement, targeting, and answer influence are separate questions. An ad shown next to an answer is a placement question, the same kind as the ads above my Dotloop results. An ad chosen because of what someone is asking about is a targeting question: OpenAI says the current conversation can inform which ad appears even with personalization off, and with it on, past chats, memory and ad interactions can too. An ad that changed what the answer said, or was written into the response, would be a different and more serious problem, and it's the one OpenAI says doesn't happen. By OpenAI's account, the conversation can shape the ad without the ad shaping the conversation.

The incentive is real here too, but it's harder to measure. OpenAI said on August 31, 2026 that ChatGPT Ads had reached a $1 billion annualized revenue run rate, and it described advertising as one part of its business alongside subscriptions, enterprise offerings and API usage. An annualized run rate projects recent revenue across a year; it isn't audited annual revenue. No public OpenAI revenue mix comparable to Alphabet's filing was verified for this article, so there's no advertising share to calculate. The figure does show an ad business large enough to create the same kind of pull, and it says nothing about what OpenAI intends to do with it. Nothing verified here shows Search or YouTube ads becoming more frequent over time, either, so the worry that the pattern will intensify stays a worry.

What a person can do about it

People have some control over the ads they see. Some only change targeting or privacy: OpenAI's Ad Controls cover personalization, dismissing ads, feedback, explanations and clearing ad data, and none of them removes ads. OpenAI says advertisers get aggregated performance reporting, not conversations, unless someone messages an advertiser through an ad. The options that remove ads, by OpenAI's account, are Temporary Chats, a Plus or Pro plan, or a more limited free no-ads mode where it's offered. On Search, Google's October 2025 design includes a control to hide the sponsored group where it has rolled out. Ad Controls and Google's hide control change what's shown or how data is used without necessarily reducing how many ads a person sees. Of the options that remove ads in ChatGPT, Plus and Pro are paid plans, and the free no-ads mode, where it's offered, comes with fewer daily messages and no access to tools like image generation, according to OpenAI's Ads in ChatGPT help page. Paying in money or usage to see fewer ads says something about what the attention is worth.

Support or obstruction

So the title stays a question. Advertising is most of how Alphabet earns its revenue, and the evidence says ads can help people at some margins. My Dotloop searches and that video still felt like moments when the ad stopped supporting the product and started competing with the reason I opened it.

A useful test is whether a particular placement helps fund the thing a person came for without becoming the main obstacle to getting it. The revenue figures show the financial pressure, but they don't establish Google or OpenAI's intentions. My two experiences show where a placement can compete with the task: above a search result and halfway through a video. An assistant raises the same question about an ad beside an answer: does it help pay for access, or compete with what the person asked for? Whether assistants become another such place remains an open concern; the evidence here doesn't settle it.
