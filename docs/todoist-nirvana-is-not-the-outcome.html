Todoist Nirvana Is Not the Outcome

By Josiah Hunter•November 11, 2026•6 min read

“Todoist nirvana” is my phrase for the mature state of my task system. It works for me: I can manage multiple aims, projects, and goals while keeping several desired outcomes in view.

That achievement matters, but it proves only that the system works for me. An organized system does not tell me whether I chose the right project, completed the important work, or produced a worthwhile result. Todoist nirvana describes the condition of the system. It is not the outcome.

My Todoist Karma screen: Enlightened, with 25,236 completed tasks. The count records completed tasks, not whether I chose the right outcomes.

What Todoist nirvana changed

Building this system changed how I approach projects. I developed habits for capturing commitments, thinking across several aims, and maintaining a broader view of work that would otherwise compete for my attention. My capacity to focus on multiple outcomes feels greater than it was before.

I cannot treat that gain as proof of objective productivity. A task manager can show that work has been named, arranged, and tracked. It cannot establish that the finished work created value.

The system does not need to prove project success to be worthwhile. It gives me a structure for approaching work. The result still depends on what I select, what I do, and what happens because I did it.

I have also felt frustrated when other people do not seem to reach a similar level of productivity. That frustration is part of my experience, not an independent measurement of their work. It shows how much I value the habits I gained while reminding me that my system is not a universal standard for how someone else should work.

When the system becomes the work

The biggest lesson arrived while I was building the system. Preparing to do something can become an activity of its own. Scheduling, refining dashboards, selecting metrics, and adjusting a workflow can consume real time and attention without completing the task underneath them.

Those activities can still be useful. A schedule can protect time. A dashboard can expose a neglected commitment. A metric can reveal whether a process is working. The problem starts when improving the representation of the work repeatedly postpones the work itself.

The productive people I notice seem to spend more of their attention on high-leverage actions, the important things that advance the aim. That is a personal observation, not a general finding about productive people. Still, it gives me a useful test for my own system work: what action will this improvement make possible?

If I cannot answer, I may be optimizing the feeling of control. A cleaner view of a project can reduce discomfort without reducing the uncertainty inside it. I can move tasks, revise labels, and redesign a dashboard while leaving the difficult decision untouched.

A mature system should make the next meaningful action easier to see. When it becomes a place to avoid that action, maturity has turned into maintenance.

Planning that leads to action

This lesson does not make planning the enemy. Research supports a gap between intention and behavior: strengthening an intention does not produce an equal change in what people actually do (Psychological Bulletin). That gap helps explain why a well-organized plan can coexist with unfinished work.

Planning can help close the gap when it connects a situation to a specific response. A recent meta-analysis of implementation intentions, often expressed as “if this happens, then I will do that,” found an average benefit across many tests. The size of the effect varied with plan design, and the researchers also detected publication bias, so the result should not become a promise that every detailed plan will work (European Review of Social Psychology).

For me, planning proves its value through behavior. It helps when it links a real situation to a specific action I will take. If it only gives me a cleaner way to describe, sort, or view the work, then I have organized the task without advancing it.

So I ask which decision or action an improvement will support, and when. A concrete answer suggests the planning is doing its job. If the answer stays abstract, I probably need to stop designing and act.

What AI can execute, and where it fails

AI makes this distinction more consequential because it can assist with portions of some computer workflows. What it can accomplish depends on the task and environment.

In the 2026 OSWorld 2.0 benchmark, computer-use agents attempted 108 long-horizon workflows, but the best evaluated configuration completed only a minority under the benchmark’s primary all-or-nothing measure. The observed failures included losing track of constraints, missing information introduced during a task, guessing instead of requesting clarification, skipping verification, and failing to recover hidden state (OSWorld 2.0).

Those results belong to the evaluated tasks and configurations. They do not establish a general percentage of computer work that AI can perform. Even measurements described as a model’s task-completion “time horizon” depend on the task, model, context, and agent setup. They measure performance on selected, unusually well-specified tasks, not how long an AI can be trusted to work autonomously in any environment (METR).

Access to AI also does not guarantee faster work. In one randomized study of experienced open-source developers using early-2025 tools, participants took longer with AI in that specific setting (METR). That result does not show that AI generally reduces productivity. It shows why the effect has to be evaluated for the people, tools, tasks, and environment involved.

A clear outcome, an important goal, and a good agent harness can improve the conditions for execution. They are not sufficient guarantees. The agent may lack a permission, misunderstand a constraint, miss a change, or produce something polished that is still wrong.

The work I still have to own

My proposal is to concentrate human attention on choosing, guiding, checking, and recovering delegated work. The appropriate controls depend on the task, stakes, permissions, context, and available checks. When I delegate part of a computer workflow, I still need to own a simple cycle:

  1. Choose an outcome worth producing.
  2. Supply the context, constraints, access, and definition of success.
  3. Set checkpoints that can reveal drift before it becomes expensive.
  4. Compare the result with appropriate ground truth.
  5. Exercise judgment about quality, consequences, and tradeoffs.
  6. Recover when the work fails, stalls, or changes the environment unexpectedly.

Ground truth means evidence that can establish whether the result is correct: a test, a source record, a reconciled total, an expected state, or a qualified person’s review. The appropriate check depends on the task and its risk. NIST’s guidance for generative AI similarly recommends evaluating accuracy, quality, reliability, and authenticity against known ground truth with suitable human and automated methods (NIST).

Verification requires more than looking at a finished artifact and deciding that it seems plausible. It has to test the properties that matter. Monitoring catches trouble during execution. Verification evaluates the result. Judgment decides whether the result is useful and acceptable. Recovery gives the system a path forward when one of those controls exposes a failure.

This is the part I do not want to delegate accidentally. AI may reduce the labor required to investigate, plan, organize, or execute some tasks, but that makes my choice of destination more important. Efficient execution of a low-value outcome is still low-value work.

Todoist nirvana remains useful to me because it supports that responsibility. It helps me see commitments, coordinate several aims, and approach projects with better habits. Its value appears when it helps me choose and complete the right work, including work that AI assists with.

The system can prepare the ground. It can never be the result.
