Apple Is Not Positioned for Future Endeavors

By Josiah Hunter•August 25, 2026•7 min read

Revised October 9, 2026 to account for the September Siri AI beta and its documented access limits. The original argument below dates from August 25.

Apple's September rollout is the material change to the argument I made in August. On September 14, Apple announced that Siri AI had begun rolling out as a beta in English in an update about its software platforms. That is real delivery progress, and stronger evidence than a promise that a user beta would arrive later in 2026.

It is still a bounded result. As checked on October 9, Apple's Siri AI support documentation describes a beta that requires supported operating systems and hardware, matching device and Siri languages, an eligible Apple Account region, enrollment, and a waitlist whose duration can vary. The page also documents English-only availability and material regional and platform restrictions, including limits for some European Union and mainland-China accounts.

Those conditions leave broad access and reliable performance open. A beta rollout cannot by itself establish adoption, quality, or parity with other systems. Nor does it establish a new hands-on verdict from me.

The original August argument needs the same date boundary. On August 25, Apple's new Siri AI was available for developer testing, while Apple's June announcement said a user beta was planned later that year. At that point, the distinction between a preview and an ordinary product was central. A preview could show direction, but it could not close a capability gap for people who did not have the product.

The concern was never that Apple could not announce a more capable assistant. It was whether Apple's operating model could turn that capability into ordinary user access quickly enough.

The operating-model risk

I do not think Apple is incapable of building good AI. My concern is that the operating model that made Apple exceptional can move at the wrong speed for a market in which models, tools, and agent systems change quickly.

Apple's own 2025 Form 10-K pairs two important premises. It describes markets shaped by rapid technological change and says successful competition depends heavily on continuing and timely product and technology introductions. The filing also says Apple designs and develops nearly the entire product solution, including hardware, operating systems, applications, and related services.

Those statements support the premises of the argument. They do not prove that integration caused a particular Siri AI delay.

End-to-end control can make an assistant more coherent. Hardware, software, permissions, services, and privacy controls can be designed as one system instead of assembled from unrelated parts. That matters for an assistant that needs personal context and the ability to act across applications.

The same control creates coordination dependencies. If the model, operating system, cloud infrastructure, privacy review, interface, device support, and regional rollout all have to meet Apple's standard before a capability becomes broadly available, the slowest dependency can determine the user's wait. In a fast-moving AI market, that can decide whether Apple's integrated answer arrives while the category is still forming or after users have developed other expectations.

That is my conditional execution-speed concern, not a measured causal finding about Apple's current process. The September beta shows that Apple is moving through those dependencies. It does not show that the dependencies no longer matter.

Partnerships qualify the concern

Apple's architecture and partnership decisions make the argument more complicated, which is what useful counterevidence should do.

Apple says its third-generation Apple Foundation Models are a five-model family custom-built in collaboration with Google. Its architecture includes models purpose-built for Apple silicon, while AFM 3 Cloud Pro uses NVIDIA GPUs in Google Cloud. That qualifies any strict version of the claim that Apple must keep every important part of its intelligence stack inside its own hardware boundaries.

It also shows that integration does not have to mean isolation. Apple can preserve a designed system while using outside infrastructure where the task requires it. The decision to extend Private Cloud Compute to NVIDIA GPUs in Google Cloud is evidence of that flexibility.

Apple's developer strategy points in the same direction. Its intelligence frameworks provide developer pathways to Claude, Gemini, and other providers that implement Apple's language-model protocol. Apple also says Xcode 27 brings models and agents from Anthropic, Google, and OpenAI into development workflows.

These are meaningful corrections. They show that Apple sees value in outside models, heterogeneous infrastructure, and a less self-contained approach. They do not show that an ordinary user can ask Siri to select the best available model for a difficult task and receive a dependable, coherent result across the system.

Developer access is not consumer capability. A framework can make outside models available to developers while Siri remains constrained by different permissions, hardware, regions, model routing, and product decisions. Apple can therefore become more open in its tools without becoming sufficiently permeable in the product people use every day.

The relevant test is what these partnerships change for users. Do they shorten the path from capability to useful, broadly available product behavior?

Privacy should constrain the design, not excuse weak delivery

Privacy is the strongest counterargument to a simple speed-first strategy.

Apple's on-device processing and Private Cloud Compute are substantive parts of its assistant design. Apple says its Private Cloud Compute models do not store or share user data, including with Apple, and that the NVIDIA-GPU implementation maintains the same privacy guarantees. Those are Apple's architectural claims, not independently measured proof of a comparative privacy advantage, so they need to remain qualified.

Still, the design problem is real. A useful assistant needs access to personal context, applications, and actions. Users should not have to surrender control of that context for better reasoning. Apple has a legitimate reason to keep permissions, data handling, and system integration under close control.

That does not require Apple to make every model, server, or capability itself. The better correction is a more permeable system: Apple defines the privacy contract, permission model, and user experience while using outside models and infrastructure when they are materially better for a task. The system should route work intelligently without turning outside capability into an obscure developer option or an emergency fallback.

Privacy can set the conditions for useful AI. It should not become a permanent explanation for why useful AI remains narrow.

The August comparison was an analogy, not a measurement

In my August 25 view, Claude Fable 5 felt like the equivalent of 120-140 IQ, while Siri felt like 65-85 at most. That comparison was subjective and non-psychometric. It was a rough way to describe the perceived difference between a system that could sustain difficult, tool-using work and an assistant that often struggled to carry an ordinary request across context and action.

Anthropic's official Claude Fable material reports product capabilities, use cases, safeguards, and task benchmarks. It does not provide a human-IQ measurement or a controlled comparison with Siri. The figures therefore should not be read as measurements, and Anthropic did not validate them.

The useful part of the analogy is the product question underneath it. Can Siri move from answering or triggering narrow commands to handling a goal that spans context, tools, and applications? The September rollout makes that question more concrete. Its announcement alone does not establish reliable cross-app performance.

What would change my conclusion

The August argument supplied the conditions for changing the conclusion. Separating availability from reliability gives us five observable tests:

  1. Siri AI is broadly available beyond limited beta access, across relevant supported users and regions.
  1. It reliably completes multi-step, cross-app work for ordinary users, carrying context from one action to the next.
  1. Outside models operate as first-class capabilities when they are the best fit, rather than remaining hidden behind developer pathways, narrow integrations, or fallback behavior.
  1. Apple's privacy guarantees remain effective when outside infrastructure is involved, without a material usefulness penalty.
  1. The intelligence layer improves at an AI-market cadence, with meaningful capability updates arriving faster than an annual platform reveal when the underlying technology demands it.

The partnerships and English beta are evidence of movement toward those results. The October 9 access documentation explains why broad delivery remains an open question. What would answer the August concern is the combination: privacy and integration preserved, outside capability useful in the ordinary product, and reliable cross-app work reaching users at the pace the technology permits.
